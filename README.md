# The Influence of Sustainable Fashion Consciousness on Consumer Purchase Behavior

## Greenwashing Skepticism, Social Identity, and Eco-Label Trust Among Urban Fashion Consumers

### Project Overview

This project investigates how **sustainable fashion consciousness influences consumer purchase behavior**, with particular focus on three important factors:

* Greenwashing skepticism
* Social identity signaling
* Eco-label trust

The study also considers practical purchasing factors such as **price, style, quality, convenience, fit, and product availability**.

The research focuses on urban fashion consumers and examines why having awareness of sustainability does not always lead to sustainable purchasing behavior.

---

## Research Aim

The main aim of this study is to explore the impact of **greenwashing skepticism, social identity signaling, and eco-label trust** on the purchase behavior of urban fashion consumers.

### Research Objectives

1. Analyse the influence of greenwashing skepticism on consumers' willingness to pay a premium price for sustainable fashion products.
2. Examine how social identity signaling affects sustainable brand choice behavior.
3. Examine the role of eco-label trust in consumers' purchase and repeat-purchase behavior of sustainably labelled fashion products.

---

## Dataset / Data Source

### Type of Dataset

This project uses a **secondary qualitative dataset** rather than collecting primary responses from consumers.

The unit of analysis is **peer-reviewed journal articles**, not individual consumers. A total of **48 articles** were selected for the final thematic synthesis.

The included literature covers different consumer groups, countries, and research methods, with a particular focus on contemporary physical and digital fashion markets.

### How the Dataset Was Obtained

The literature search was conducted in **June 2026** using the following academic databases:

* Scopus
* Web of Science
* ScienceDirect
* Emerald Insight
* Taylor & Francis Online
* SpringerLink
* Wiley Online Library
* MDPI

**Google Scholar** was additionally used for index checking and reference chaining. Only journal articles were used as the main evidence base.

### Search Strategy

A representative search query used in the research was:

```text
("sustainable fashion" OR "ethical fashion" OR "green apparel")
AND
(consumer OR purchase OR buying)
AND
(greenwashing OR skepticism OR trust OR identity OR "eco-label")
```

The search was restricted to:

* Publication years: **2022–2026**
* Language: **English**
* Publication type: **Peer-reviewed journal articles**
* Topic: Sustainable fashion, apparel consumption and related consumer behavior
* Evidence: Studies containing findings relevant to the research objectives

## Data Collection and Preparation

A **structured data extraction form** was used to collect relevant information from each article.

The extracted information included:

* Bibliographic details
* DOI
* Country / market setting
* Consumer group and sample
* Research method
* Key concepts
* Reported outcomes
* Author-reported limitations
* Initial codes
* Evidence categories
* Final themes

Relevant evidence from the articles was recorded and paraphrased into a coding sheet. Multiple codes could be assigned to the same piece of evidence where concepts overlapped.

---

## Data Analysis

### Reflexive Thematic Analysis

The main analytical technique used was **qualitative thematic synthesis / reflexive thematic analysis**.

The purpose was not to calculate statistical effects or build a predictive statistical model. Instead, the analysis identified:

* Common patterns
* Relationships between concepts
* Contradictory findings
* Consumer motivations
* Consumer barriers
* Recurring meanings across studies

### Coding Process

The articles were reviewed multiple times and initial codes were developed from recurring evidence.

Examples of initial codes included:

* Price barriers
* Need for evidence
* Peer approval
* Label confusion
* Trusted certification
* Repeat purchase
* Style compromise
* Limited availability
* Selective sustainable purchasing

These codes were then grouped into broader categories and developed into final themes.

---

## Key Themes Identified

Four major themes emerged from the analysis.

### Theme 1: Practical Purchasing Conditions

**Conscious values are filtered through price, style, convenience and availability.**

Although consumers may be concerned about sustainability, practical considerations such as affordability, design, quality, fit, convenience and availability can prevent sustainable intentions from becoming actual purchases.

---

### Theme 2: Greenwashing Skepticism

**Greenwashing skepticism weakens trust and willingness to pay, but does not always stop consumption.**

When consumers perceive environmental claims as vague, exaggerated or unsupported, their trust in the brand decreases. This can also reduce willingness to pay a premium.

However, skepticism does not necessarily stop consumers from purchasing when fast-fashion alternatives are cheaper, more attractive or more convenient.

---

### Theme 3: Social Identity and Peer Signaling

**Sustainable fashion can work as a form of social identity and peer signaling.**

Consumers may use sustainable fashion to communicate:

* Personal values
* Environmental responsibility
* Ethical identity
* Group membership
* Social belonging

Social media, influencers and peer communities can strengthen this effect.

---

### Theme 4: Eco-Label Trust

**Eco-label trust reduces uncertainty when labels are clear, familiar and verifiable.**

Eco-labels can provide information about aspects such as materials, production and sustainability standards.

However, labels are more effective when they are:

* Easy to understand
* Independently verified
* Recognised by consumers
* Transparent
* Easy to compare

Confusing or excessive labels can instead increase uncertainty.

---

## Conceptual Framework

The research proposes an integrated framework connecting:

```text
Sustainable Fashion Consciousness
             ↓
Greenwashing Skepticism  |  Social Identity Signaling  |  Eco-Label Trust
             ↓
Practical Filters (Price • Style • Fit • Quality • Convenience • Availability)
             ↓
Consumer Purchase Behavior
```

The framework therefore suggests that sustainability consciousness does not automatically result in sustainable purchasing. Instead, purchasing behavior is influenced by trust, identity and practical market conditions.

---

## Main Findings

The analysis indicates that:

1. **Sustainability awareness alone is not enough** to guarantee sustainable purchasing.
2. **Price, style, quality, convenience and availability** strongly influence the translation of sustainable values into purchasing behavior.
3. **Greenwashing skepticism reduces consumer trust** and can reduce willingness to pay a premium.
4. Consumers may continue purchasing less sustainable products despite skepticism when those products are affordable and convenient.
5. **Social identity can encourage sustainable brand choices**, particularly when sustainable behavior is visible and supported by peers.
6. **Credible eco-labels can reduce uncertainty** and support consumer confidence and repeat purchasing.
7. Sustainable purchasing is better understood as a combination of interacting conditions rather than as a simple sustainable/unsustainable consumer classification.

## Target Population

The underlying studies mainly examined **urban and digitally connected fashion consumers**, including:

* Generation Z
* Millennials
* Working-age consumers

The included literature covered studies from **Asia, Europe, North America and Latin America**.

## Ethical Considerations

No primary participants were recruited and no personal consumer data were collected.

The research relied entirely on published academic literature. Ethical considerations therefore focused on:

* Accurate representation of published findings
* Appropriate citation
* Maintaining the context of original studies
* Distinguishing original findings from the researcher's interpretation

---

## Limitations

The study has several limitations:

* It relies on secondary published evidence.
* The original studies used different samples, methods and measures.
* Much of the underlying evidence is cross-sectional and self-reported.
* Direct behavioral and repeat-purchase evidence is relatively limited.
* Only English-language studies published between 2022 and 2026 were included.
* The definition of an "urban consumer" was not consistent across all studies.
* Thematic analysis involves researcher interpretation.
* The findings should not be interpreted as causal statistical relationships.

## Conclusion

This project demonstrates how secondary academic evidence can be systematically collected and analysed to understand sustainable fashion consumer behavior.

The findings suggest that sustainable fashion consciousness is an important starting point, but consumer behavior depends on a combination of **trust, social identity and practical purchasing conditions**.

For sustainable fashion to translate into actual consumer behavior, products need to be credible, affordable, attractive, accessible and supported by clear and verifiable sustainability information.
